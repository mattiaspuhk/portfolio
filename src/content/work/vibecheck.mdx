## Problem

Visa asked: what's a valuable problem to solve for Gen Z in the financial space? It added two conditions: consider the AI experience paradigm, and consider the current ecosystem and partners.

The brief named a user but not a customer. Three parties had different stakes:

- **Gen Z cardholders** want to enjoy spending now, as trends push them to, without losing financial security.
- **Issuing banks** own the account and the customer, and earn from lending.
- **Visa** runs the network and gains from trusted, growing card use.

Our first hard question on Monday was whose interest we were designing for. As a product engineer, I treat that as the first scoping decision: the answer sets the owner, the data you can use and the success metric.

The problem changed three times in three days. Each change made it narrower and easier to build against.

| Day | What we believed | What moved us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wed | Priorities are the things people love to spend on: travel, festivals, fitness | "Values" felt moralising and was hard to read from spending. We also added one wishlist across all shops, so the check has something concrete to compare against. |
| Tue | Trend and social-media spending crowds out what Gen Z actually care about | The root definition forced us to name an owner: the card issuer, with Visa as network partner. |
| Mon | AI could capture a user's values and steer spending toward them | Our rich picture showed the tension was at the moment of spending, not in the credit product. |
| Mon | Gen Z credit products (BNPL, credit cards) are the problem space | Credit explained how Gen Z pay, not why they overspend. |

We used Soft Systems tools to get there: a rich picture of the whole problem, CATWOE, root definitions, then eight "How Might We" questions and a dot vote. Two of those HMWs became design rules: show the gap without shaming, and let users define what matters without feeling profiled.

The target we committed to for the rest of the week:

> Show Gen Z what an impulse buy costs them in terms of what they really care about, right before they pay.

## Constraints

The most important design choices came from what the system could and couldn't know. Each constraint below changed where the product runs or what it is allowed to do.

| Constraint | What it meant for the design |
| --- | --- |
| Card data shows where you spent, not what you bought | Item details come from the checkout basket (in an app, at online checkout or inside an AI agent), not from card transactions. |
| Card authorisation happens after the decision is made | The check must run before payment, inside the checkout flow. A statement insight is too late. |
| Inferring interests from spending counts as profiling under UK GDPR | Consent is a required step in setup. The AI suggests priorities; the user confirms, edits or deletes them. |
| Nudges that feel judgemental get ignored | The check never blocks a purchase. It always offers buy, save to wishlist or wait 24 hours. |
| Social media shapes both impulses and aspirations | The system can't decide what is a "real" priority. The user does. |
| Visa is a network, not a bank | The issuer owns the customer and holds the money. Visa supplies the feature ready-made, so banks add it without building it. |
| More shopping is done by AI agents | The same priority check attaches to an agent's payment token through Visa's agent spend controls. |

## Approach

VibeCheck is three components that share one engine: a comparison of what you're about to buy against what you said matters. We called it a platform, not an assistant.

| Component | What it does | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Priority profiler | Turns past spending and loose interests into a confirmed priority list and one wishlist | AI analysis of transaction history, a short guided chat, and a save button that works in any shop or app |
| Checkout check | Shows what a non-essential purchase costs in the user's own priorities and gives an opinion | AI compares the basket with the priority list and wishlist just before payment |
| Agent guardrails | Makes AI shopping agents buy in line with the cardholder's priorities | Priority-based rules on the agent's payment token, plus wishlist price watching across shops |

The checkout check is the core of the product. In the prototype it runs like this:

1. The user reaches checkout in any app or shop.
2. Before payment, VibeCheck compares the basket with their priorities and wishlist.
3. It shows the trade-off in their own terms: "this £45 = 2 nights in Lisbon", or "this is on your wishlist and £10 cheaper than last week".
4. Two optional views add context: **Forecast** (how the purchase is likely to feel later) and **Worth It?** (the user's own ratings of similar past purchases).
5. The user picks buy, save to wishlist, or wait 24 hours. Buying is always one tap away.

### From storyboard to system design

We modelled the system before building it, so the UI, the diagrams and the AI-generated code would agree. The rule was simple: every screen maps to a use case and a class, or something changes.

**Use case diagram.** One system boundary with three packages, matching the three components. The cardholder and the AI shopping agent are primary actors. The issuer bank, merchant, AI model provider and Visa network are secondary actors. Three modelling choices carry product decisions:

- Forecast, Worth It?, Save to wishlist and Wait 24 hours `<<extend>>` the base "Check purchase before paying" use case. The model itself says the check never blocks: buying stays the normal path.
- "Compare basket with priorities and wishlist" is `<<include>>`d by both the human checkout and the agent's purchase. One engine serves both, which ties agent guardrails to the core idea.
- Setting up priorities always includes consenting to spending analysis, so the GDPR requirement is part of the model, not a footnote.

**Class diagram.** Core classes include Cardholder, PriorityProfile, Priority, Wishlist, WishlistItem, Basket, CheckoutCheck, Decision and PurchaseRating. Rating a past purchase became its own use case because Worth It? needs that data to exist.

The diagrams were written in PlantUML, so the same text went straight into Kiro as part of the build spec.

### Build strategy

We built the riskiest, most valuable slice first: the checkout sheet. It is the climax of the demo, and every other screen exists to feed it.

**Where to differentiate.** Our Wardley map placed card data, payment rails and AI models as commodities to reuse. The differentiation is the priority profile and the translation of a price into the user's own priorities. That is where we spent design effort.

{/* TODO(mattias): confirm the Wardley map conclusion matches your team's map. */}

**Build order.**

1. Seed data for one persona, Maya: her priorities, wishlist, four past ratings and a £45 basket.
2. The checkout sheet and its variants, clickable on their own.
3. Onboarding and consent.
4. The everyday app: home, priorities, wishlist, history and rating.
5. Agent screens, only once steps 1–4 worked.

**Spec-driven prototyping.** We used Kiro, an agentic AI development tool, from a written spec rather than ad-hoc prompts. The spec covered screens, copy, states and seed data, plus the two PlantUML diagrams. The target was a clickable mobile prototype as a single-page app in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

**Demo path.** We scripted the 2-minute video through six screens: confirm priorities, reach checkout, see the trade-off, choose to wait 24 hours, get the reminder, and find the item on the wishlist.

## Decisions

Most of our decisions traded control for trust. We kept the user in charge because a tool that feels like a judge gets switched off.

<Decision title="Never block a purchase" options={["Hard spending limits", "Never block a purchase"]} chose="Never block a purchase">
Blocking reads as judgement. Friction plus choice keeps people using it.
</Decision>

<Decision title="Priorities, not values" options={["A values-based profile", "Priorities, not values"]} chose="Priorities, not values">
Priorities map to real purchases and feel less moralising.
</Decision>

<Decision title="AI suggests, user confirms" options={["A fully inferred profile", "AI suggests, user confirms"]} chose="AI suggests, user confirms">

## When it broke

We appointed a UML checker. We built a matrix of screens against use cases and classes. It surfaced nine gaps, for example:

- No `AgentRule` class for the agent screens
- No `currentPrice` or status on `WishlistItem` for price-drop alerts
- No `remindAt` time for the 24-hour wait
- One `consentGiven` flag where the UI asked for three separate opt-ins

## Next

The prototype tests desirability. Before building further, I'd prove the check changes behaviour and that basket data is actually reachable.

| Metric | What it tells us |
| --- | --- |
| Worth It? ratings after a check vs without | Whether people regret fewer purchases, the core outcome |
| Share of wishlist items bought from savings, not BNPL | Whether planned buying replaces instant credit |
| Mute or dismiss rate on the check | Early warning that it feels judgemental |
| Taps to save an item to the wishlist | Whether capture is truly one tap |
| Retention of cardholders using the feature | The issuer's business case |

Next steps:

- Run a quick test: five people name their top three priorities, sort their last ten transactions into priority, trend or essential, then react to one mock trade-off.
- Spike where basket data really comes from: in-app checkout, merchant integration or agent context.
- Test two tones of the trade-off message against the mute rate.
- Answer the open HMW: how to make the check worthwhile for merchants, so they don't resist the friction.
