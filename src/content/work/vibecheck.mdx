## Problem

Visa's brief was open. What's a valuable problem to solve for Gen Z in the financial space? It asked us to consider the AI experience and the current ecosystem of partners.

By the third day we had narrowed it to one target.

> Show Gen Z what an impulse buy costs them in terms of what they really care about, right before they pay.

## Constraints

Most of the design came from five facts about what the system can and can't know.

- Card data shows where you spent, not what you bought.
- Card authorisation happens after the decision to buy is already made.
- Inferring someone's interests from their spending counts as profiling under UK GDPR.
- Nudges that feel judgemental get ignored.
- Visa is a network, not a bank. The issuer owns the customer and holds the money.

## Approach

VibeCheck compares what you're about to buy with the priorities you've set and your wishlist. The checkout check is the core of it, and in the prototype it runs like this.

1. Before payment, VibeCheck compares the basket with the user's priorities and wishlist.
2. It shows the trade-off in their own terms, like "this £45 = 2 nights in Lisbon" or "this is on your wishlist and £10 cheaper than last week".
3. Two optional views add context. Forecast shows how the purchase is likely to feel later, and Worth It? shows the user's own ratings of similar past purchases.
4. The user picks buy, save to wishlist, or wait 24 hours. Buying is always one tap away.

## Decisions

<Decision title="Check before payment, from the basket" options={["Insights on the statement afterwards", "A check inside checkout, from basket data"]} chose="A check inside checkout, from basket data">
Card data has no line items, and by the time a payment is authorised the decision is already made. A statement insight would only need data the issuer already has, but it arrives too late to change anything. So the check runs inside checkout and reads the basket the shop shares, in an app, at online checkout or inside an AI agent. The cost is that it only works where a shop shares its basket.
</Decision>

<Decision title="AI suggests, the user confirms" options={["Infer the profile from spending", "AI suggests priorities, the user confirms each one"]} chose="AI suggests priorities, the user confirms each one">
Inferring interests from spending is profiling under UK GDPR. So consent is a required step in setup, and the AI only suggests priorities. The user confirms, edits or deletes each one. Setup gets longer, and that's the price.
</Decision>

<Decision title="Never block a purchase" options={["Hard spending limits", "Never block, always offer a choice"]} chose="Never block, always offer a choice">
Nudges that feel judgemental get ignored, and a blocked payment is as judgemental as it gets. The check always offers buy, save to wishlist or wait 24 hours, and buying stays one tap away. That means the check can only persuade. It never stops anyone.
</Decision>

<Decision title="Issuer owns, Visa supplies" options={["A standalone Visa app", "A feature issuers add to their own apps"]} chose="A feature issuers add to their own apps">
Visa is a network, not a bank. The issuer owns the customer and holds the money. So Visa supplies the feature ready-made and banks add it without building it, and "by Visa" keeps Visa's name in front of the cardholder. Visa gives up owning the relationship with the user.
</Decision>
